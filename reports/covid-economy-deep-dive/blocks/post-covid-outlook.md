The post-COVID economy is best described as a lower-growth, higher-friction baseline rather than a return to the old normal. In the IMF April 2024 vintage, growth after the rebound was around 3.2–3.5%, below the 2000–2019 average of about 3.8% [S1]. The IMF's inflation outlook showed disinflation underway but not an immediate return to the prior price environment [S3].

The World Bank's January 2025 outlook adds the broader context: policy uncertainty, trade-policy shifts, geopolitical tensions, inflation risks, climate-related disruptions, and weak medium-term growth can reinforce one another [S4]. These pressures do not all have the same timing, but they create a planning environment in which a single optimistic forecast is fragile.

## Planning implications

| Decision area | Pre-COVID default | Post-COVID adjustment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Growth | Extrapolate a central trend | Use scenarios and downside triggers |
| Supply | Optimize for cost and speed | Price resilience and alternate paths |
| Finance | Treat rates as relatively stable | Stress-test refinancing and cash flow |
| Workforce | Track headcount and output | Track hours, skills, retention, and job quality |
| Risk | Separate operational and macro risk | Model their interaction |
| Investment | Commit around a base case | Stage commitments and preserve option value |

These are not arguments for permanent pessimism. They are arguments for a wider definition of preparedness. When the operating environment is more volatile, flexibility can be economically productive even when it looks less efficient in a calm period.
