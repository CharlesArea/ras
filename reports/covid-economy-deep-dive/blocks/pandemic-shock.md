The 2020 contraction was unusual because it combined a public-health emergency with an enforced reduction in physical activity. Demand disappeared in contact-intensive sectors, firms paused investment, households faced income uncertainty, and supply networks were disrupted at the same time. That combination made the recession feel operationally different from a conventional demand slowdown.

The IMF April 2024 statistical series records a 2.7% contraction in world real GDP in 2020 [S1]. Other contemporaneous institutions used different vintages and revision paths; the World Bank's early assessment described an even deeper global contraction before later data revisions [S6]. The exact percentage therefore depends on the measurement vintage, but the direction and scale are not ambiguous: 2020 was a global recession with a sharp break in activity.

The price environment initially moved in the opposite direction from the later inflation episode. The World Bank describes an early collapse in demand and oil prices, followed by a reopening in which demand returned faster than supply in many markets [S5]. In practical terms, organizations had to survive first a revenue and mobility shock, then an input-cost and financing shock.

## Why the shock propagated

The pandemic transmitted through several channels:

- **Demand:** households and firms postponed spending, travel, hiring, and investment.
- **Mobility:** rules and health concerns limited the sectors that depend on physical contact.
- **Supply:** factories, ports, logistics networks, and labor availability were disrupted.
- **Finance:** uncertainty increased the value of cash and reduced the willingness to take risk.
- **Expectations:** firms could not easily tell whether a disruption would last weeks, quarters, or years.

The important lesson is that the shock was not only “less GDP.” It was a simultaneous test of operational flexibility and financial endurance.
