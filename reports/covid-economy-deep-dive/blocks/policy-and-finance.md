The policy environment changed as the crisis moved through its phases. In 2020, the central concern was preventing a temporary shutdown from becoming a permanent collapse in jobs, firms, and financial intermediation. During the rebound, the concern shifted toward keeping demand and supply from becoming dangerously misaligned. Once inflation persisted, policy had to become more restrictive even though some parts of the economy were still repairing pandemic damage [S3][S5].

## The financing channel

Higher borrowing costs affect the economy with a lag. They make it harder for firms to refinance, for households to absorb higher rents or mortgage costs, and for governments to fund new support or investment. The same rate that helps cool demand can also slow construction, business expansion, and the replacement of equipment.

That trade-off matters for the post-COVID baseline because the economy entered the inflation adjustment with uneven balance sheets. Some firms had survived through emergency borrowing; some households had lost income or accumulated arrears; some governments had less room for a second large intervention. The recovery therefore depended not only on the policy rate but on the distribution of debt and cash-flow sensitivity.

## Uncertainty as a cost

The World Bank's medium-term outlook identified policy uncertainty, trade-policy shifts, geopolitical tensions, inflation, and climate-related disruption as continuing headwinds [S4]. Even when a risk does not materialize, uncertainty can delay investment and make firms prefer flexibility over capacity. That behavior is rational for an individual firm but can reduce aggregate productivity if it persists.

The practical result is a more scenario-driven economy: businesses and governments need to plan for different combinations of growth, inflation, finance, and supply reliability rather than a single central forecast.
