The pandemic made fragility visible. Before 2020, many organizations treated long, optimized supply chains and low inventory as evidence of efficiency. During the shock, the same structure could become a single point of failure. The World Bank's inflation analysis connects the later price episode to the combination of reopening demand and supply disruption [S5]. Its January 2025 outlook continues to identify trade and policy uncertainty among the constraints on the medium-term expansion [S4].

This does not mean that every firm should abandon specialization or hold excessive inventory. It means resilience has become an explicit design variable. The relevant question is not “Can the system be optimized under normal conditions?” but “How does the system behave when transport, labor, energy, or a critical supplier becomes unavailable?”

## Four operating changes

- **Redundancy has value:** a second supplier or route can be worth paying for when failure is costly.
- **Visibility is strategic:** firms need earlier signals about bottlenecks, inventories, labor, and demand.
- **Flexibility competes with efficiency:** modular capacity and adaptable contracts can reduce the cost of being wrong.
- **Scenario planning becomes continuous:** the range of plausible outcomes is wider when policy and geopolitics are unstable.

These are analytical implications, not universal prescriptions. The right mix depends on sector, geography, balance sheet, and customer tolerance. The broad conclusion is that resilience moved from an operational concern to a board-level economic variable.
