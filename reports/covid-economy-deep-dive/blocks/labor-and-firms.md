The labor-market evidence shows why GDP alone is not enough to describe the pandemic economy. The ILO estimated that 8.8% of global working hours were lost in 2020, equivalent to 255 million full-time jobs. Global employment fell by 114 million compared with 2019, while the employment shortfall relative to a no-pandemic scenario was estimated at 144 million jobs [S2].

These are not interchangeable measures. Working hours capture reduced schedules and inactivity; employment captures whether people had jobs; the counterfactual shortfall captures the jobs that might otherwise have existed. Together they show a shock that affected both people who lost employment and people who remained attached to work but with fewer hours or less income.

## Uneven exposure

The ILO emphasized that the effects were highly uneven. Contact-intensive services, workers with less protection, informal workers, younger workers, women in exposed sectors, and small firms could face a sharper loss of income or operating capacity than the global average suggested [S2].

For firms, survival often depended on liquidity, access to support, the ability to move activity online or redesign delivery, and the duration of restrictions. A temporary closure could become a balance-sheet problem if fixed costs continued while revenue disappeared. The recovery could then restore demand without restoring every firm that had been present before the shock.

## Measurement implication

A serious recovery dashboard therefore needs at least three layers: aggregate output, labor-market attachment, and the health of the firm base. A high-level growth number can improve while household security and productive capacity remain impaired.
