The phrase “before COVID” can make the pre-pandemic economy sound stable and uniform. The data show something more nuanced: the world economy was expanding, but the rate of expansion was already weakening by 2019. In the IMF April 2024 historical series, world real GDP growth was 3.3% in 2016, 3.8% in 2017, 3.6% in 2018, and 2.8% in 2019 [S1].

That pattern matters because it changes the counterfactual. The question is not whether COVID-19 interrupted an economy that would certainly have continued at 3.8% indefinitely. It is whether the shock pushed an already cooling system onto a different path. The evidence supports the second interpretation: the pandemic created a much larger discontinuity than the pre-2020 slowdown, but it interacted with weaker momentum that was already visible.

## What the baseline offered

Before the pandemic, decision-makers could generally plan around a world of positive growth, moderate inflation, functioning cross-border supply networks, and a policy environment in which the central challenge was often how to lift demand or productivity. That baseline was imperfect, but it provided a useful assumption of continuity.

The baseline also concealed distributional differences. A global average can rise while some economies, sectors, firms, or households are already under pressure. This becomes important later: when the pandemic arrives, the ability to absorb a shutdown depends on fiscal space, access to finance, sector exposure, job protection, and the ability to shift activity away from physical contact.

The pre-COVID period should therefore be read as a reference point, not a golden age. It gives the report a comparison for growth and inflation, but it does not tell us that all workers, firms, or countries were equally secure.
