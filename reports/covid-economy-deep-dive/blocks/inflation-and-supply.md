The price story changed direction twice. Early in the pandemic, demand collapsed and oil prices fell, pushing inflation down. As economies reopened, demand returned faster than supply in many markets. Bottlenecks, energy prices, food pressures, and the time required to restore capacity then moved inflation sharply higher [S5].

The IMF's April 2024 outlook placed global headline inflation at 6.8% in 2023, 5.9% in 2024, and 4.5% in 2025 [S3]. The direction is downward, but the adjustment is not instantaneous. An economy can move away from the peak while still operating above the inflation environment that households and firms had treated as normal. Earlier IMF analysis also compared the post-shock inflation path with a pre-pandemic norm around 3.5% [S7].

## Why supply mattered

In a conventional demand recession, weak spending can reduce pressure on prices. The pandemic was more complicated: policy support and reopening demand arrived while some production, transport, and labor channels remained constrained. The result was a mismatch between what people wanted to buy and what the economy could deliver quickly.

This changed business planning. Input costs could move before selling prices adjusted. Inventory decisions became strategic rather than purely operational. Long lead times increased the cost of being wrong. Wage negotiations became more sensitive to the loss of purchasing power. Households faced a squeeze between higher prices and uncertain income.

## The lasting policy lesson

Inflation made the recovery harder to steer. Policymakers had to balance the need to preserve employment and investment against the risk that continued demand support would keep supply pressure alive. Businesses had to plan for financing costs and prices that were less predictable than in the pre-COVID baseline.
